A quick timeline

One way to understand the history of neuroscience is to watch the main questions evolve: How do neurons grow? → How does the brain store memory? → How do circuits compute? → How does the brain navigate, recognize, feel, and change?

1950s–60sGrowth & memoryRita Levi-Montalcini on nerve growth factor; Brenda Milner on memory after hippocampal damage.
1970s–90sDevelopment & cortexCarla Shatz on neural development; Patricia Goldman-Rakic on prefrontal cortex and working memory.
1990s–2000sSelective systemsNancy Kanwisher on face selectivity; Eleanor Maguire on hippocampus and navigation; Ann Graybiel on habits.
2000s–presentMaps, circuits & interventionMay-Britt Moser on grid cells; Helen Mayberg on depression circuits; Cori Bargmann and Eve Marder on circuit logic.

🧠
Memory systems · Neuropsychology

Brenda Milner

Brenda Milner helped establish one of the most important ideas in all of cognitive neuroscience: memory is not a single thing. Different brain systems support different kinds of memory.

Why she matters

By studying patients with brain lesions—especially the famous patient H.M.—Milner showed that severe damage to the medial temporal lobes can destroy the ability to form new episodic memories while leaving other abilities relatively intact.

Classic paper

Scoville & Milner (1957), “Loss of recent memory after bilateral hippocampal lesions.” The paper became foundational because it tied a specific memory impairment to a specific brain region.

Core insight

The hippocampus is crucial for forming new declarative memories, but procedural learning can survive. This opened the door to the distinction between episodic, semantic and procedural memory systems.

Figure to remember

The classic contrast: H.M. cannot remember recent events, yet can improve on tasks like mirror tracing. The “impairment + preserved skill learning” pattern is the key figure in words.

Development · Neurobiology

Rita Levi-Montalcini

Rita Levi-Montalcini co-discovered nerve growth factor (NGF), one of the molecules that transformed developmental neuroscience.

Why she matters

Her work showed that neural development is not only about fixed anatomy—it is also regulated by chemical signals that support neuronal growth and survival.

Classic paper

Her early chick embryo and tumor-graft experiments with collaborators laid the path to NGF. Start by reading a good review of NGF history alongside the original line of work.

Core insight

Neurons compete for trophic support. Growth factors help determine whether neurons survive, grow and connect properly during development.

Figure to remember

The classic developmental picture: neurite outgrowth increasing in response to NGF. It is one of the images that made molecular control of neural growth vivid.

Prefrontal cortex · Working memory

Patricia Goldman-Rakic

Goldman-Rakic made the prefrontal cortex central to modern thinking about working memory, executive control, and persistent neural activity.

Why she matters

She built a bridge between single-neuron physiology, primate behaviour and cognition. Her work helped show how prefrontal neurons can maintain task-relevant information across delays.

Classic paper

Goldman-Rakic (1995), “Cellular basis of working memory.” A classic entry point because it links behaviour, cortex and microcircuit computation.

Core insight

Working memory is not magic—it emerges from organized neural circuits that can sustain information over short periods, especially in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

Figure to remember

Delayed-response tasks and neurons that keep firing during the delay. That sustained activity over time became one of the iconic figures of cognitive neuroscience.

Navigation · Spatial coding

May-Britt Moser

May-Britt Moser co-discovered grid cells, giving neuroscience one of its most elegant coding schemes for space.

Why she matters

The Moser lab helped explain how the brain represents position and movement, extending earlier hippocampal place-cell work into a broader navigation system.

Classic paper

Hafting, Fyhn, Molden, Moser & Moser (2005), “Microstructure of a spatial map in the entorhinal cortex.”

Core insight

Grid cells fire in multiple locations arranged in a hexagonal lattice. This suggested that the brain contains an internal coordinate system for navigation.

Figure to remember

The famous firing map with regular hexagonal fields. It is one of the most recognizable visual figures in modern systems neuroscience.

Development · Plasticity

Carla Shatz

Carla Shatz transformed our understanding of how neural circuits are wired during development, especially through activity-dependent mechanisms.

Why she matters

Her work showed that neural activity is not just a consequence of brain wiring—it is also one of the forces that helps create and refine that wiring.

Classic paper

Start with her work on visual system development and activity-dependent refinement, then move to later papers on unexpected roles of immune molecules such as MHC class I in the brain.

Core insight

Early spontaneous and sensory-driven activity helps sculpt neural circuits. Development is therefore both genetic and activity-dependent.

Figure to remember

Ocular dominance / retinogeniculate refinement diagrams: messy early connectivity gradually becomes precise through patterned activity.

fMRI · Face perception

Nancy Kanwisher

Nancy Kanwisher is widely associated with the discovery and development of the concept of the fusiform face area (FFA).

Why she matters

Her work became central in debates about modularity: does the brain contain regions specialized for particular categories such as faces, places and bodies?

Classic paper

Kanwisher, McDermott & Chun (1997), “The fusiform face area: a module in human extrastriate cortex specialized for face perception.”

Core insight

Some cortical regions show reliable selectivity for specific categories. That gave a concrete neural foothold for questions about recognition and domain specificity.

Figure to remember

The brain image highlighting the fusiform face area and the contrast between stronger response to faces than to other visual categories.

Navigation · Structural MRI

Eleanor Maguire

Eleanor Maguire helped make the hippocampus famous beyond the lab through work on spatial memory, scene construction and London taxi drivers.

Why she matters

Her work connected real-world expertise with measurable brain differences, showing how experience and cognition can be reflected in brain structure and function.

Classic paper

Maguire et al. (2000), “Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers.”

Core insight

The hippocampus is deeply involved in building and using internal spatial representations. Long-term navigational expertise appears linked with hippocampal structural differences.

Figure to remember

The comparison showing posterior hippocampal differences in taxi drivers versus controls—one of the most cited images in experience-dependent plasticity discussions.

Basal ganglia · Habits

Ann Graybiel

Ann Graybiel made the basal ganglia central to our understanding of habits, action sequences and chunking.

Why she matters

Graybiel helped move habit from a vague behavioural label to a concrete circuit-level problem involving corticostriatal loops and action patterns.

Classic paper

Start with review-style entries on habits and the evaluative brain, then read task-based papers on basal ganglia activity during action chunking.

Core insight

As behaviours become habitual, the brain appears to represent them differently, often showing chunking of action sequences into more automatic units.

Figure to remember

The pattern where neural activity brackets the start and end of a learned action sequence—a signature image for habit chunking.

Model organisms · Circuits & genes

Cori Bargmann

Cori Bargmann showed how an apparently simple organism like C. elegans can reveal deep principles linking genes, circuits and behaviour.

Why she matters

Her work made small nervous systems intellectually powerful: with defined circuits and genetics, they let us ask sharp mechanistic questions about behaviour.

Classic paper

Start with her seminal studies on olfaction and neural control of behaviour in C. elegans, then move to later work on social behaviour and circuit logic.

Core insight

Behaviour can be dissected from genes to neurons to circuits. Simpler systems can reveal organizing principles that generalize far beyond the worm.

Figure to remember

A compact circuit diagram showing identified sensory neurons feeding into behavioural outputs. This is the power of tractable model organisms.

Circuits · Neuromodulation

Eve Marder

Eve Marder changed how neuroscientists think about circuit robustness, variability and neuromodulation.

Why she matters

Her studies of small rhythmic circuits, especially the stomatogastric ganglion, showed that similar outputs can arise from different parameter combinations.

Classic paper

Begin with her reviews on central pattern generators and degeneracy in neural circuits. They are unusually readable and conceptually rich.

Core insight

Neural circuits are not rigid machines. The same behaviour can be produced by different internal configurations, which has huge implications for modelling and interpretation.

Figure to remember

A small rhythmic circuit with multiple ionic and synaptic parameters producing a stable output—illustrating degeneracy and robustness.

Affect · Stress · Psychiatry

Huda Akil

Huda Akil has been hugely influential in affective neuroscience, especially in linking stress, pain, emotion and psychiatric biology.

Why she matters

Akil helped create bridges between basic neuroscience and mental health, arguing that emotional and psychiatric phenomena can be studied rigorously at molecular, circuit and systems levels.

Classic paper

Start with broad reviews on stress, neuroplasticity and the biology of mood disorders, then move into specific translational work.

Core insight

Stress and affect are not peripheral to brain science. They are central biological processes with measurable circuit, molecular and behavioural signatures.

Figure to remember

Conceptual maps linking stress systems, reward, affect and resilience. Her influence is especially strong at the level of integrative frameworks.

Clinical neuroscience · Brain stimulation

Helen Mayberg

Helen Mayberg helped make depression research more circuit-based, especially through work on deep brain stimulation (DBS) and brain-network models of mood disorders.

Why she matters

She reframed treatment-resistant depression as a disorder of dysfunctional circuits that may, in some cases, be modulated with targeted intervention.

Classic paper

Mayberg et al. (2005), “Deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression.” A landmark in translational and interventional neuroscience.

Core insight

Psychiatric illness can be studied as network dysfunction. This helps connect imaging, symptoms and treatment into a shared framework.

Figure to remember

The network diagram linking subgenual cingulate and broader mood circuits, plus early clinical response trajectories in DBS studies.

Plasticity · Enriched environment

Marian Diamond

Marian Diamond became one of the public faces of neuroplasticity through work showing that the brain can change in response to enriched experience.

Why she matters

Her work was crucial in popularizing the idea that the brain is dynamic rather than fixed. That influence reached far beyond academia.

Classic paper

Start with her enriched-environment studies in rodents and then read later summaries on cortical plasticity.

Core insight

Experience can alter the brain’s structure and function. Plasticity is not limited to early development.

Figure to remember

The classic comparison between animals in enriched versus less enriched environments, often linked to cortical or synaptic differences.

More names to keep exploring

Additional women neuroscientists worth reading

If you want to expand the list, continue with these names: Susan Greenfield (public neuroscience, consciousness), Catherine Dulac (social behaviour, circuits), Lisa Feldman Barrett (affect, constructed emotion), Mu-ming Poo’s collaborators such as Nancy Ip (neurotrophins, development), Kafui Dzirasa’s collaborators like Kay Tye (circuits of emotion), Vivian Tabar (neural stem-cell translation), and Flora Vaccarino (developmental neurobiology).

Best use of this list

Treat this as a “where next?” section. Once someone has understood the main landmark figures above, these names help them branch into social neuroscience, affect, neurodevelopment, and translational neuroscience.

How to explore

Use review papers first, then move to a scientist’s signature empirical paper, then a lecture or interview. This three-step route is the fastest way to gain both depth and context.

Good habit

Whenever you read one landmark paper, ask: What question made this paper necessary? What method made it possible? What changed after it?

Core ideas

The figures and concepts to remember

If your audience remembers these concepts, they will already understand a surprising amount of modern neuroscience history.

Multiple memory systems

From Brenda Milner: memory is not unitary. Episodic/declarative memory and procedural learning can dissociate.

  • Key image: H.M. impairment + mirror-tracing improvement
  • Takeaway: cognition is fractionated across systems

Nerve growth factor

From Rita Levi-Montalcini: neurons require molecular growth support.

  • Key image: neurite outgrowth / survival
  • Takeaway: development is chemically regulated

Persistent activity

From Patricia Goldman-Rakic: neurons can maintain information across a delay.

  • Key image: prefrontal firing during working-memory delay
  • Takeaway: working memory has circuit mechanisms

Grid-cell lattice

From May-Britt Moser: spatial coding can be periodic and geometrically structured.

  • Key image: hexagonal firing map
  • Takeaway: the brain has an internal metric for space

Activity-dependent wiring

From Carla Shatz: neural activity helps sculpt developing circuits.

  • Key image: connectivity refinement
  • Takeaway: development is not purely hard-wired

Category selectivity

From Nancy Kanwisher: specialized cortical responses can emerge for certain categories.

  • Key image: FFA activation
  • Takeaway: cortex may show functional specialization

Experience-dependent plasticity

From Eleanor Maguire and Marian Diamond: experience can shape brain structure or function.

  • Key image: taxi-driver hippocampus / enriched environments
  • Takeaway: the brain changes with use

Habit chunking

From Ann Graybiel: repeated action sequences become represented in compressed, efficient forms.

  • Key image: start–end bracketing activity
  • Takeaway: automaticity has circuit signatures

Degeneracy & robustness

From Eve Marder: similar outputs can arise from different circuit parameter sets.

  • Key image: multiple solutions, same behaviour
  • Takeaway: biology is flexible, not one-to-one
Study plan

A reading path for beginners

If someone comments BRAIN and wants to go deeper, this is a good progression: start broad, then read classic papers, then watch lectures, then compare themes across scientists.

Stage 1

Get the story first

Start with short biographies and broad explainers. The goal is not technical mastery yet. The goal is to know who changed what.

  • Read one short profile per scientist.
  • Write down the main discovery in one line.
  • Group each scientist by theme: memory, development, navigation, clinical, etc.
Stage 2

Read one classic paper each

Do not try to read everything. For each scientist, read one flagship paper or review and answer three questions.

  • What was the question?
  • What method was used?
  • What changed after this paper?
Stage 3

Compare ideas across fields

This is where real understanding starts. Compare how different scientists approached the brain.

  • Milner vs Kanwisher: cognition from lesions vs fMRI
  • Shatz vs Marder: circuits developing vs circuits stabilizing
  • Moser vs Maguire: navigation cells vs real-world navigation expertise

If you are from psychology

Begin with Milner, Kanwisher, Maguire, Graybiel and Mayberg. These names connect well to cognition, behaviour and clinical questions.

  • Good entry papers: H.M., FFA, taxi drivers
  • Then expand to working memory and depression circuits

If you are from biology

Begin with Rita Levi-Montalcini, Carla Shatz, Cori Bargmann and Marian Diamond.

  • Focus on development, molecules and model systems
  • Then move into systems-level questions

If you are from engineering / data

Begin with Goldman-Rakic, Moser, Marder, Kanwisher and Mayberg.

  • These names link naturally to models, signals and computation
  • Then connect to imaging and circuit intervention

Final takeaway

The cleanest way to understand scientific influence is not to memorize names. It is to ask: What invisible question did this person make visible? Brenda Milner made memory systems visible. Rita Levi-Montalcini made growth factors visible. Patricia Goldman-Rakic made working memory circuits visible. May-Britt Moser made spatial codes visible. Carla Shatz made developmental refinement visible. Nancy Kanwisher made functional selectivity visible. That is why these scientists matter.

If you are sharing this with your audience, you can frame it like this: “Read this slowly. Pick two scientists. Read one classic paper each. Then tell me which question you want to understand next.”
